DBT Skills Sampler for Prescribers

Not DBT — DBT-informed. Four teachable skills for emotion-dysregulation moments, sized for a med-management visit, to bridge patients toward (or between) full skills training.

Scope — be honest about what this is

  • Who benefits: patients with emotion dysregulation — borderline traits, PTSD with impulsive discharge, anger dyscontrol, urge-driven behaviors — especially those waiting for or unable to access full DBT.
  • What this is: individual crisis-survival and emotion-regulation skills, taught deliberately, practiced between visits.
  • What this is not: comprehensive DBT (which requires skills group, individual therapy, phone coaching, and a consultation team). For recurrent suicidal or self-injurious behavior, these skills are a bridge alongside an active referral to a DBT program and a completed Safety Plan — never a substitute.
  • Say the frame out loud: "These are skills borrowed from the strongest therapy we have for intense emotions. I'm giving you the starter set while we get you connected to the full program."

STOP — the impulse circuit-breaker

StepTeach it as
SStop"Freeze. Don't move a muscle. The urge is not the boss yet."
TTake a step back"Physically leave the room if you can. Distance from the trigger is half the skill."
OObserve"Name what's happening — outside you and inside you. 'He said X, my chest is tight, I want to throw the phone.'"
PProceed mindfully"Now ask: what do I actually want to happen here? Act on the goal, not the urge."

Best for: anger explosions, impulsive texts/quitting/spending, the fight-with-spouse moment. It buys the seconds between urge and action where every other skill lives.

TIPP — dropping physiological arousal fast

For the 8-out-of-10 states where thinking skills can't land yet — panic surges, rage, overwhelming urges. TIPP changes body chemistry first so the mind can follow.

SkillHow
TTemperatureCold water on the face (or a cool pack over eyes/cheeks) while holding breath briefly triggers the dive reflex and slows heart rate.
IIntense exercise10–15 minutes of hard effort — burpees, fast stairs, sprint-walk — burns off the adrenaline the emotion mobilized.
PPaced breathingExhale longer than inhale (below). Portable, invisible, works anywhere.
PPaired muscle relaxationTense a muscle group on the inhale, release completely on a slow exhale, moving through the body.
Medical screening before teaching Temperature: the dive reflex causes rapid heart-rate deceleration — screen for cardiac conditions, arrhythmias/bradycardia, beta-blockers, and consult before recommending it in those patients (intense exercise also warrants the usual cardiovascular common sense). For these patients, lead with paced breathing and paired relaxation instead. This screening note belongs in your chart when you teach it.

Opposite Action — when the emotion doesn't fit the facts

The teach: "Every emotion comes with an action urge. When the emotion is justified by the facts, follow it. When it isn't — or acting on it makes life worse — do the opposite, all the way, and the emotion itself weakens."

EmotionUrgeOpposite action
DepressionWithdraw, stay in bedGet active, approach people (this is BA's engine — cross-link the BA module)
Anxiety/fear (unjustified)Avoid, escapeApproach the feared situation, repeatedly, without safety behaviors
AngerAttack, cut offGently avoid or be civil; unclench the body; find one thing to half-respect in the other person's position
Shame (when the "offense" wouldn't get them rejected by people whose values they share)Hide, concealShare it with a safe person; keep the head up
Guilt (when no wrong was done)Over-apologize, over-repairStop apologizing; act confident in the decision

The two checks before prescribing it: (1) Does the emotion fit the facts? If it does — the guilt is earned, the fear is realistic — the skill is problem-solving or repair, not opposite action. (2) Is the patient willing to do it all the way? Half-hearted opposite action (approaching while white-knuckling) rehearses the fear instead of extinguishing it.

Paced breathing — the 60-second prescription

Exhale longer than inhale to engage the parasympathetic brake: in through the nose ~4 seconds, out slowly ~6, for two minutes. Practice twice daily when calm — the skill must be over-learned in peacetime to be available in war. Do it with the patient once in session; a skill demonstrated is remembered, a skill described is not.

"This is the one skill you can use in the middle of a meeting, in the car, at 3 a.m. Nobody can see you doing it. But it only works in a crisis if you've practiced it two hundred boring times first — that's the deal."

Teaching a skill in 5 minutes (the repeatable loop)

  1. Link to their episode: "Walk me through Tuesday night — what happened right before you punched the wall?" Find the moment the skill would have slotted in.
  2. Teach ONE skill — the one matching that moment. One per visit; a menu teaches nothing.
  3. Rehearse in session: run the breathing together; walk STOP through the Tuesday scenario out loud.
  4. Assign with specificity: when, where, cue ("phone in the other room BEFORE the call with your ex — that's Take a step back, pre-loaded").
  5. Review next visit first, exactly like the BA review: done → reinforce and refine; not done → task-design problem, shrink and re-anchor.
Español: STOP → "Pare. Dé un paso atrás. Observe. Proceda con cuidado." · Respiración: "Inhale por la nariz contando cuatro, exhale despacio contando seis. La exhalación más larga que la inhalación — eso es lo que calma el cuerpo." · Acción opuesta: "Cuando la emoción no corresponde a los hechos, haga lo contrario de lo que la emoción le pide — completamente, no a medias." · El trato: "Esta destreza solo funciona en una crisis si la ha practicado muchas veces cuando está tranquilo."
