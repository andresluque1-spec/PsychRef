Billing-critical visit opener — first sentence of every HPI

Claims get denied without this, regardless of note quality. State modality of visit and both locations before anything else.

Telehealth (patient at home): "Patient is a [marital status] [demographic] with [diagnoses] who presents for telemedicine follow-up with me in the office and patient at home." Telehealth (anywhere other than home): "…presents for telemedicine follow-up with me in the office and patient in a private place." In person: "…presents for in-person follow-up with me in the office."

Master therapy paragraph — supervisor's standard

Lives in the Treatment Plan. Written fresh every visit — never carried forward — and always ends with the minutes statement that anchors the 90833.

Insight-oriented, [solution-focused / CBT-focused / supportive] therapy used to address mood and anxiety. We explored ___ since last visit. I helped the patient understand ___. I taught the patient ___ (skill/concept). I challenged the patient with the concept of ___. I laid framework for future therapy sessions by ___. I gave homework to ___. [X] minutes spent in therapy separate from med management.

The Core 3 + CBT-I — chosen from your actual panel

Across your caseload, the recurring picture is moderate-to-severe MDD+GAD, frequent alcohol and nicotine ambivalence (often minimized or denied), chronic insomnia as a maintaining factor (F51.05), caregiver and adjustment stress, and trauma histories in patients who want present-focused work only. Four modalities cover ~95% of that ground in a 16-minute 90833 window:

Brief CBT is the workhorse for acute anxiety spirals and avoidance-driven depression. Motivational Interviewing is the tool for everything the patient is ambivalent about — drinking, smoking, therapy referrals, medication adherence. Solution-Focused is built for time-limited contact and fits caregiver stress, high-functioners, and "med management only" patients. CBT-I addresses insomnia as a distinct clinical target — it's often the keystone intervention that allows the depression and anxiety to resolve (sleep loss drives them both). Your trauma-informed grounding cards remain the cross-cutting safety layer for any visit that destabilizes.

The one rule for 30-minute visits: one modality, one skill, one piece of homework per visit. Depth over breadth — a completed 5-minute intervention documents and works better than three half-started ones. CBT-I is often a 3–4 visit sequence (stimulus control, then sleep restriction, then cognitive restructuring), so plan accordingly; it's not a one-visit intervention.

Brief CBT — the depression & anxiety workhorse

For your F32/F33 + F41.1 core, avoidance-driven functional decline, and chronic insomnia. CBT works in 15-minute doses when — and only when — you target one thought or one behavior per visit and always close with written homework.

Who it fits on your panel

  • Depression with avoidance/anhedonia — behavioral activation first, cognitive work second. A patient at PHQ-9 ≥ 15 usually can't out-think depression; they have to out-act it.
  • Anxiety with catastrophic or ruminative worry — verbal thought record, decatastrophizing, worry time.
  • Chronic insomnia (F51.05) — stimulus control + worry time; behavioral conditioning is almost always a maintaining factor in insomnia older than a few months.
  • Social/work avoidance — graded exposure, one rung per visit.

The 16-minute skeleton (inside a 30-min follow-up)

Med management first, then a clean transition: "Now let's spend some time on the skills side of this."

Min 0–2Bridge + homework review. "Last time you agreed to ___ — how did it go, or what got in the way?" Never skip this; it's what makes homework real.
Min 2–5Pick one target. "Walk me through one specific moment this week when it hit hardest." One situation, not the whole week.
Min 5–12The intervention. Verbal thought record, BA prescription, or one exposure rung — pick one, finish it.
Min 12–14Assign homework. Specific, scheduled, small. Have the patient say it back.
Min 14–16Summary + confirm. "Today we caught the thought ___, and you're trying ___ before next visit. Sound right?"

Core move 1 — the 5-minute verbal thought record

No worksheet needed. Six questions, in order. Write the automatic thought down verbatim — you'll reuse it in your note.

1 · Situation"Give me one specific moment this week when the anxiety spiked. Where were you, what was happening?"
2 · Thought"What went through your mind right at that moment?"
3 · Emotion + intensity"And what did you feel — and how strong, 0 to 100?"
4 · Evidence"What tells you that thought is true? … And is there anything from that day that doesn't quite fit it?"
5 · Alternative"Knowing both sides — what's a fairer, more accurate way to say it?"
6 · Re-rate"Sitting with that new version, where's the feeling now, 0 to 100?"

The cardinal rule: the patient generates the alternative thought, not you. If they're stuck, ask "What would you tell a friend who had this exact thought?" — never supply the answer.

Core move 2 — the behavioral activation prescription

For anhedonia and avoidance. Frame it as an experiment on mood, never as exercise, productivity, or "should."

  • Pick one: "What's one thing you've dropped that used to matter to you, even a little?"
  • Shrink it: cut it to a 10-minute version. Walking the dog once, not "getting back in shape."
  • Schedule it: exact day and time. "Tuesday after dinner" — vague homework is unfinished homework.
  • Pre-empt the obstacle: "What's most likely to get in the way? And what's your workaround?"
  • Set the frame: "This is data collection. Rate your mood before and after, and we'll look at what happened."

Core move 3 — CBT-I mini-toolkit

You can deliver the two highest-yield CBT-I components in one visit each. Pairs naturally with hypnotic-sparing conversations.

Stimulus control (visit one)

  • Bed is for sleep only — no phone, TV, or worrying in bed
  • Awake ~20 min? Out of bed, dim light, boring activity, return when drowsy
  • Fixed wake time seven days a week — this is the anchor, not bedtime
  • No daytime naps while retraining

Cognitive arm (visit two)

  • Worry time: 15 scheduled minutes in the early evening; worries earlier in the day get parked on a list
  • Reframe sleep effort: "Trying hard to sleep is the one thing guaranteed to prevent it"
  • De-catastrophize the bad night: "You've functioned on bad sleep before — uncomfortable, not dangerous"

Homework menu — patient-facing wording

  • Thought log: "Catch three thoughts this week — jot down the situation, the thought, the feeling. Just notice, don't fix anything yet."
  • BA step: "One 10-minute walk Tuesday and Thursday after dinner — not for exercise, as an experiment on your mood. Rate it before and after."
  • Behavioral experiment: "You're predicting ___. Test it once this week and write down what actually happened."
  • Worry time: "15 minutes at 7 PM is your worry appointment. Anything earlier goes on the list for later."
  • Exposure rung: "Do the bottom item on our list twice this week. Rate the distress before, during, and after — you're watching for the 'during' number to drop."

What to look for

Progress markers

  • Patient labels their own distortions unprompted ("I know, I'm catastrophizing again")
  • Homework completion — the single best predictor of brief-CBT outcome
  • Distress ratings drop within and across exposures
  • PHQ-9/GAD-7 move in step with behavior change, not just med changes

Red flags — pivot signals

  • Homework never done twice running → stop assigning, switch to MI; it's ambivalence, not skill deficit
  • Hopelessness blocks all cognitive work → drop to pure BA; action before insight
  • Trauma content keeps surfacing → stabilize, ground, and make the therapy referral concrete
  • You catch yourself disputing the thought for the patient → back to questions

Motivational Interviewing — for everything they're ambivalent about

Alcohol and nicotine minimization, declined therapy referrals, spotty adherence, drinking against the antidepressant. The core inversion: the patient voices the argument for change, never you. The moment you argue for change, they argue against it.

The spirit and the four processes

Spirit: partnership (two experts in the room — you on medicine, them on their life), acceptance, compassion, evocation (the reasons for change already exist inside the patient; your job is to draw them out).

Four processes: Engage → Focus → Evoke → Plan. In a 16-minute window you'll usually do two of these well, not all four. Evoking across two or three visits before planning is normal and correct.

The 16-minute skeleton

Min 0–3Engage. One open question, then reflect. No agenda yet. "How have things been going with everything on your plate?"
Min 3–6Focus — ask permission. "Would it be okay if we talked about the drinking for a few minutes?" Asking permission is itself the intervention — it disarms defensiveness.
Min 6–13Evoke. Importance/confidence rulers, then mine for change talk (DARN below). Elaborate every scrap of it: "Tell me more about that part."
Min 13–16Plan only if ready. If change talk is flowing: menu of options, patient picks one small step. If not: summarize both sides, end on the change side, leave the door open. That's still a complete MI session.

Question bank — eliciting change talk (DARN)

Desire"What would you want to be different about your drinking, if anything?"
Ability"If you did decide to cut back, how would you go about it? What's worked before, even briefly?"
Reasons"What are the not-so-good things about how it's going right now?"
Need"Honestly, where does this sit on your priority list? How important is it to you?"
The rulers"On a 0–10 scale, how important is making this change? … And separately, how confident are you that you could, if you decided to?"
The backward question"You said a 4 — why a 4 and not a 2?" (Always ask downward — it makes them argue for change. "Why not higher?" makes them argue against it.)
The key question (after a summary)"So, given all of that — where does that leave you? What do you think you'll do?"

The reflection ladder

Aim for roughly two reflections per question you ask. Reflections are statements, voice dropping at the end — not questions.

  • Simple: restate or lightly rephrase. "It's been a rough month."
  • Complex: continue the paragraph — name the unspoken feeling or meaning. "Part of you wonders whether the drinking is making the anxiety worse, not better."
  • Double-sided: "On one hand, the wine is the only thing that shuts your brain off at night — and on the other, you're waking up more anxious than you went to bed." Always end on the change side.
  • Amplified (use sparingly): slightly overstate the sustain talk so the patient corrects toward change. "So the drinking really isn't affecting anything at all." Requires a solid alliance and a completely non-sarcastic tone.

What to listen for

Change talk → elaborate it

  • DARN statements, plus the stronger CAT: Commitment ("I'm going to…"), Activation ("I'm ready to…"), Taking steps ("I already cut back on weeknights")
  • Response: reflect it, ask for more, put it in your end-of-visit summary — change talk the patient hears back is change talk reinforced

Sustain talk & discord → soften

  • Sustain talk ("It's how I unwind") is normal ambivalence — reflect it without amplifying, then pivot to the other side
  • Discord (arguing with you, interrupting, shutting down) is feedback about the relationship — drop the agenda, reflect, re-ask permission
  • Watch your own righting reflex: the urge to warn, list consequences, or convince. It reliably produces the opposite of what you want

Classic traps: question–answer volleys (feels like an interrogation), the expert trap (prescribing the fix), the labeling trap (arguing about the word "alcoholic"), and premature focus (pushing your topic before they've been heard).

Homework menu — MI-consistent (patient chooses)

  • Pattern diary: "Would you be willing to just track it for a week — no changes required — so we can look at the pattern together?"
  • The noticing task: "Notice one moment this week when you reach for a drink, and what's happening right before. That's all."
  • One call: "Not committing to therapy — just one phone call to whichever name on the list looks least bad. Which one would you pick?"
  • Menu close: "A few ways we could go: I hand-pick a therapist, I resend the list, or we table it until next visit. What sounds most doable?"

Solution-Focused — built for exactly your time constraint

SFBT was designed for brief, intermittent contact — which is precisely a med-management follow-up cadence. Best for caregiver and adjustment stress, high-functioning patients, and "medication only" patients who'd reject anything that smells like therapy. You're not solving the problem; you're amplifying what already works.

Who it fits on your panel

  • Caregiver overload — spouse with cognitive decline, parent in the ICU, adult children in crisis. The problem won't resolve; coping capacity can grow.
  • High-functioning professionals — goal-oriented patients who respond to concrete, forward-facing structure and would bristle at exploratory work.
  • "Med management only" patients — SFT doesn't feel like therapy from the inside, so it lands where CBT homework would be refused.
  • Adjustment reactions — situational distress with an intact baseline underneath.

The 16-minute skeleton (EARS — the follow-up engine)

The miracle question is a first-visit tool; use it once. EARS + scaling is what runs every follow-up after that.

Min 0–2Elicit. "What's been better — even slightly — since we last talked?" Not "how are you." The question presupposes something improved.
Min 2–6Amplify. Whatever surfaces, dig in: "How did you do that? What was different that day? Who else noticed?"
Min 6–10Reflect + scale. "Where are you today, 1–10? … What's keeping it from being a point lower?" (That second question surfaces coping they're not crediting themselves for.)
Min 10–14Start again — next small step. "What would a [current number + 0.5] look like this week? What's the smallest visible sign?"
Min 14–16Compliment + summary. One specific, evidence-based affirmation of something they actually did, then the recap.

The full question ladder (first visit or reset)

1 · Best hopes"What are your best hopes for our work together — what would make these visits worth your time?"
2 · Miracle question"Suppose tonight, while you sleep, this problem resolves — but you don't know it happened. Tomorrow morning, what's the first small thing you'd notice that tells you something changed?"
3 · Pieces already present"When was the last time you saw even a small piece of that morning happen for real?"
4 · Scale it"If 10 is that morning and 1 is the worst it's been — where are you today?"
5 · Coping (for the defeated patient)"Given everything you're carrying, how have you kept going at all? What does that take?"
6 · First step"What's one small thing this week that would tell you the number moved, even half a point?"

Handling "I don't know": wait three full seconds — most patients answer into the silence. If not: "Suppose you did know — what might you say?" It works surprisingly often.

Homework menu — SFT-style tasks

  • Formula first-session task: "Between now and next visit, notice what's happening in your life that you want to keep happening. That's the whole assignment."
  • Exception watch: "Notice the moments the problem is a little smaller than usual, and what's different about those moments."
  • Do-more-of: once an exception is identified — "You said Tuesday was better because you got outside before the boys woke up. Do that on purpose twice this week."
  • Pretend task: "Pick one morning and act as if the miracle already happened. Don't tell anyone which morning — see who notices something's different."

What to look for

Progress markers

  • Patient arrives reporting exceptions unprompted
  • Scaling numbers creep upward — or the patient starts defining the numbers in behavioral terms without help
  • Language shifts from problem-talk ("everything's falling apart") to solution-talk ("Tuesday went okay because…")
  • They take ownership of the next step before you ask

Red flags — pivot signals

  • Flat "nothing's better" across multiple visits with worsening scores → this may be depression needing BA/CBT, not a coping problem
  • Any safety content → drop the frame entirely, full risk assessment
  • You're cheerleading past real distress → validate first ("this is genuinely hard"), then pivot to coping
  • Miracle question during acute crisis → wrong tool, wrong moment; use the coping question instead

CBT-I — the keystone intervention for your panel

Chronic insomnia (F51.05) is not a symptom of depression; it's a maintaining factor that keeps it locked in place. When you address insomnia directly, mood and anxiety often improve without additional psychotherapy. CBT-I is the gold standard and the one non-pharmacological intervention that outperforms most hypnotics in long-term outcomes.

Who it fits on your panel

  • F51.05 (Insomnia due to another mental disorder) — when MDD or GAD has disrupted sleep for 6+ months.
  • Early morning awakening (3–5 AM) — classic depression phenotype; stimulus control and cognitive work are the moves.
  • Nighttime worry spirals, racing thoughts — patient "can't turn off my brain"; this is anxiety-driven insomnia and calls for worry time scheduling inside the sleep protocol.
  • Sleep effort ("I try too hard") — paradoxical: trying to sleep prevents sleep; this is pure cognitive CBT-I work.
  • Medication-resistant insomnia — patient on two or three hypnotics with minimal benefit; behavioral conditioning is almost always a maintaining factor by this point.

The 3–4 visit sequence (not a one-visit intervention)

CBT-I works best as a series. Plan this into your follow-up cadence; it's not a single 90833 skill.

Visit 1Psychoeducation + stimulus control rules. Explain the "window of sleep drive" and what's blocking it (sleep effort, bed behavior, napping). Assign: bed = sleep only, up if awake >20 min, fixed wake time.
Visit 2Review stimulus control compliance + cognitive work. Address "trying hard to sleep" belief. Teach decatastrophizing: "One bad night ≠ dangerous. You've functioned on poor sleep before." Assign: one thought log per week about sleep beliefs.
Visit 3Sleep restriction (optional, advanced). If stimulus control is helping: match time-in-bed to actual sleep time, then gradually expand. This requires more structure and patient buy-in. Alternative: worry time if nighttime thoughts are the barrier.
Visit 4+Maintenance + troubleshooting. Sleep is often the first thing that regresses when stress returns. Regular check-ins on sleep metrics, and quick re-teaching of whichever component was most helpful.

Core move 1 — Stimulus control (visit 1, laser-focused)

The single most effective component of CBT-I. Patients are often shocked how much bed behavior shapes sleep.

Psychoeducation frame"Sleep drive builds up over the day. The problem is we're spending too much time in bed before we're actually sleepy — so the bed becomes a place of worry, not sleep. We need to reset that."
The rules (write them down)"Bed is for sleep only — no phone, TV, reading, worrying, or 'trying to fall asleep.' If you're awake 20 minutes, get up, go to a different room, do something boring in dim light until you feel drowsy. Then return to bed. This might happen 3–4 times a night at first — that's normal and means it's working."
The anchor"The single most important rule: wake up at the SAME time every day, even weekends, even if you barely slept. This sets your sleep drive clock. Everything else — when you go to bed, naps, exercise timing — follows from this one anchor."
The no-nap rule"No daytime naps while we're retraining your sleep. Even 20 minutes will reset your sleep drive and make tonight harder."
Homework frame"This might feel worse for the first week or two. You'll be more tired because we're concentrating your sleep into a shorter window. That's the goal — it intensifies sleep drive so you actually sleep when you're in bed. Stick with it for at least two weeks before we evaluate."

Core move 2 — Cognitive restructuring for sleep effort (visit 2)

The thought "I have to make myself fall asleep" is paradoxically the barrier to sleep. This is where CBT-I gets philosophical and sometimes surprising to patients.

The paradox"The more you try to fall asleep, the less likely you are to fall asleep. Your brain can't force sleep — it can only allow it. Trying is the opposite of allowing."
1 · What's the fear?"What worries you most about a night of poor sleep? What do you tell yourself will happen?"
2 · Reality-test it"Have you had nights of poor sleep before? What actually happened? Did you crash the next day? Did you function, even if uncomfortable?"
3 · Decatastrophize"Let's separate the worst-case (which hasn't happened) from what's more likely. Bad sleep is uncomfortable, but it's not dangerous. Your brain is resilient."
4 · The acceptance piece"What if tonight you had 4 hours instead of 8? You'd function. It would be harder, but possible. That's true whether you're 'trying' or not. The trying doesn't actually help — it just adds anxiety on top of the insomnia."
Homework"Jot down one sleep fear each night, then answer: 'Have I survived poor sleep before? What actually happened?' This is data collection, not positive thinking."

Core move 3 — Worry time scheduling (visit 2 or 3, if nights are full of racing thoughts)

Many insomnia cases have an anxiety layer that stimulus control alone won't touch. Nighttime is when the worry machine starts.

The frame"When you lie in bed, your brain has nothing to do but think. And we tend to think about worries and problems at night. We're going to move that to a scheduled time during the day — 'worry time' — so it's not hijacking your sleep."
The prescription"Pick 15 minutes in the early evening — say 6 or 7 PM — as your designated worry appointment. Write down the concerns you have. Then tell yourself: 'I'll think about this carefully at 6 PM.' When worries pop up during the day or at night, jot them on a list and tell yourself 'that's for 6 PM.' At 6 PM, spend 15 minutes actually worrying — no phone, no distractions, just the concerns. After 15 minutes, stop. The worries will still be there tomorrow if needed."
Why it works"You're not suppressing worry — that doesn't work. You're containing it. Your brain gets that worries are important, but they have their time, not 3 AM."
Homework"Worry time at [specific time], every day. Write down worries that come up outside that window. Review in two weeks."

What to look for

Progress markers

  • Patient reports following the rules (bed only for sleep, up if awake >20 min)
  • Sleep efficiency improves — time asleep / time in bed ratio climbs toward 85%+
  • Daytime mood and energy improve before sleep hours increase (this is classic CBT-I — behavior change precedes symptom resolution)
  • Patient spontaneously reports "it's so boring getting up at 2 AM, but I sleep better now"

Red flags — troubleshooting points

  • Rules not followed (bed still used for phone/worry) → clarify that stimulus control is non-negotiable; motivation is about boredom, not willpower
  • Worse insomnia in week 1–2 → normal; sleep drive intensifies before sleep consolidates. Reassure and ask for 2-week commitment
  • No improvement after 3–4 weeks → add cognitive component (worry time, decatastrophizing) or consider sleep restriction if motivation is high
  • Patient quits early saying "it's not working" → it's ambivalence or activation barrier; switch to MI to explore resistance

Trauma-Informed & Grounding — the cross-cutting safety layer

Not a competing modality — this runs underneath any of the other three. Use it proactively with patients who want present-focused work only, and reactively the moment a session destabilizes, regardless of what you were working on.

Who it fits on your panel

  • Trauma history, present-focused preference — patients who explicitly don't want to "go back there." Stabilization and coping come first; historical content is never required for progress.
  • Dissociation or flooding mid-session — can happen during CBT, MI, or SFT work when a question lands closer to raw material than expected. This panel is your in-the-moment toolkit.
  • Hyperarousal / hypervigilance — patients who startle easily, scan the room, or describe feeling "keyed up" without a clear trigger.
  • Shame-driven presentations — self-blame, "something's wrong with me" — externalizing language does real work here.

When a session destabilizes — the in-the-moment sequence

This isn't a scheduled 16 minutes — it's what you reach for the moment you notice a shift. Stop whatever else you were doing.

NoticeName it out loud, gently. "I want to pause for a second — I notice something shifted just now. Are you here with me?" Trust your read; you don't need certainty to check in.
OrientBring them back to the room. Say their name. State the date, the location, your name and role. "You're here with me, in my office/on this call, it's [day]. You're safe right now."
GroundRun the 5-4-3-2-1 sequence (below) or a body-based anchor — feet on the floor, hands on the chair arms, one slow breath.
Check capacityAsk, don't assume. "Do you want to keep going, or would it help to stay here for a bit before we continue?" Handing back control is itself the intervention.
DecidePivot or close. If grounded and willing: return to lighter content or move to med management for the remainder. If not fully settled: stay in stabilization and end the therapy portion there — that's a complete, billable visit.

Core move 1 — 5-4-3-2-1 grounding (in-session script)

Say each prompt slowly, and wait. Rushing defeats the purpose.

5 · See"Look around the room and name five things you can see. Take your time — just say them out loud as you notice them."
4 · Touch"Now four things you can feel — the chair under you, your feet on the floor, your hands together."
3 · Hear"Three things you can hear right now, even quiet sounds."
2 · Smell"Two things you can smell, or would smell if you noticed."
1 · Taste"One thing you can taste, even just your own mouth."
Close"Good. Take one more breath. Where are you now, on a scale of calm to overwhelmed?"

Core move 2 — Window of Tolerance psychoeducation

Normalizes the response as physiology, not failure. Draw or describe three zones if helpful.

The frame"There's a zone where your nervous system can think clearly and feel things without being overwhelmed — that's your window. Trauma makes that window narrower, so smaller things can push you outside it — either revved up and flooded, or shut down and foggy. What just happened to you is your body doing exactly what it's designed to do when it senses too much. It's not weakness, and it's not you doing something wrong."
Naming the state"Right now, does it feel more like your system sped up — heart racing, hard to sit still — or slowed down — foggy, distant, hard to feel your body?" (Answer tells you whether to use an activating or calming grounding technique next time.)

Core move 3 — titration & pendulation

For approaching material that's close to the edge of tolerance without overwhelming. Small doses, deliberate retreat.

  • Dose it: "We don't need the whole story today — just tell me as much as feels manageable, and we'll stop there."
  • Pendulate back on purpose: "Let's step away from that for a second — tell me about something that felt steady or good this week." Then return only if the patient is ready.
  • Externalize the material: "the anxiety," "the flashback," "that part of the memory" — not "you." Separates identity from symptom.
  • Collaborative pacing check-in: "You're in the driver's seat here — tell me to slow down or stop any time, and I will, no explanation needed."

Safe/calm place & containment — building portable resources

Safe/calm place imagery

  • "Think of a place, real or imagined, where you feel completely safe or at ease. Describe it to me — what you see, hear, feel there."
  • Build it in detail over 3–5 minutes; anchor with a word or image the patient can recall alone
  • Practice retrieving it briefly at the end of session so it's rehearsed, not just described

Containment exercise

  • "Imagine a container — a box, a safe, anything sturdy. When something feels like too much right now, you can put it in the container and close the lid. It's not gone, it's just not due right now."
  • Useful for material that surfaces but can't be processed in the time remaining
  • Explicitly not suppression — frame as "later, on purpose" not "never"

Homework menu — patient-facing wording

  • Grounding practice: "Practice the 5-4-3-2-1 once a day when you're calm — not just in a crisis. It works better in an actual moment of need if it's already familiar."
  • Window noticing: "Notice one moment this week when you felt pushed outside your window — what happened right before?"
  • Safe place rehearsal: "Visit your calm place for two minutes before bed, just to keep it fresh."
  • Body check-ins: "Twice a day, just notice: revved up, shut down, or in the window? No need to change it — just notice."

What to look for

Progress markers

  • Patient self-initiates grounding without prompting ("I need a second, let me do the thing")
  • Faster return to baseline within a session over time
  • Patient can name their own state (revved up / shut down / in the window) accurately
  • Willing to approach slightly more material session over session — titration is working

Red flags — escalate or refer

  • Dissociation doesn't resolve by end of session → do not send the patient home alone; involve support, consider higher level of care
  • Destabilization is increasing in frequency or severity → this needs trauma-specialist referral, not more grounding
  • Any safety content surfaces during flooding → drop this frame entirely, full risk assessment (see Crisis Reference)
  • Patient dissociates and cannot orient even with full sequence → err toward ending the visit and arranging same-day follow-up or higher level of care

Session Structure — the connective tissue under every modality

Not a technique you choose — it's what carries every technique above. Opening well, staying on time, redirecting without feeling abrupt, and handling the moments that don't fit any protocol at all.

Who it fits on your panel

  • Every visit, every modality — this is the frame, not the content.
  • New or unstable patients — clear structure builds trust faster than any specific technique.
  • Sessions that go sideways — off-topic tangents, doorknob disclosures, unexpected emotion.
  • Alliance maintenance — small structural moves (feedback, recap) are cheap and compound over time.

Opening a session well

Ten seconds of agenda-setting saves five minutes of drift later, and signals the visit has a shape.

Orient + invite"Before we get into the medication side — what's most on your mind today? I want to make sure we get to it."
Name the plan"Today I want to check on the sertraline and also pick back up on the sleep work from last time — does that match what you need, or is something else more pressing?"
Following up on last visit"Last time you left saying ___ — how's that sitting with you now?"

Homework review — closing the loop without shame

If done"You actually did it — tell me how it went." (Let them report before you comment; their read matters more than yours.)
If not done"That's okay — a lot gets in the way. What happened, or what made it harder than it sounded in here?" (Never "why didn't you." Curiosity, not accountability-as-punishment.)
Reframing a "failed" attempt"You tried it and it didn't go the way we planned — that's still useful information. What did we learn?"

Handling silence

Silence is data, not a problem to fix immediately. Most clinicians break it too soon.

  • Let it sit: count five seconds in your head before you speak. Most patients are formulating, not stuck.
  • If it continues: "Take your time — I'm not going anywhere." Removes the pressure that's often causing the silence itself.
  • If it feels like shutdown, not thought: "I notice it's gotten quiet — is this a thinking pause, or did something just get harder to say?"
  • Never fill it with your own content to relieve your own discomfort — that's the single most common structural error.

What to say when a patient cries

The instinct to resist"Take whatever time you need — there's no rush." (Do not immediately hand a tissue and change the subject; let the emotion complete.)
Naming it gently"Something real just came up. Can you tell me what's underneath the tears, or is it okay to just sit with it a moment?"
When it passes"That took something to let show. Thank you for letting me see it." (Validates without over-processing if time is short.)

Redirecting an off-track session

Redirect the topic, not the person — the difference is everything.

Gentle bridge"This is important, and I don't want to rush it — can I flag that we also need a few minutes for the medication side today, and come back to this next time if we run short?"
Naming the drift kindly"We've drifted a bit from where we started — which is fine — but I want to check: is this where you need us to be right now?"
Time-boxing out loud"Let's give this five more minutes, and then I want to make sure we land the plan for the week."

The doorknob disclosure — SI or big news at minute 29

The most common structural crisis: significant disclosure right as the visit is ending.

  • Never rush a safety disclosure to protect the schedule. "I'm really glad you told me that, even now — let's take the time we need." The visit's length is now determined by clinical need, not the original slot.
  • For non-safety big news arriving late: "That's significant, and it deserves real time. I have a few minutes now — can we go deeper into it next visit, or does it need to be sooner than that?"
  • Name the pattern if it recurs: "I've noticed some of our most important stuff tends to come up right as we're wrapping — no judgment, just wondering if starting there next time might serve you better."

Difficult in-session moments — phrase bank

Cuts across every modality above. The move is almost always: name it, don't push past it, hand back some control.

"I don't want to talk about it""That's completely okay — you don't owe me that today. Is there something else that would be more useful to spend our time on?"
Anger directed at you"I hear that you're frustrated with me right now. I'd rather you say it directly than sit with it — what's not working?" (Do not get defensive; the willingness to say it to your face is a sign of alliance, not its absence.)
Silent shutdown, won't re-engage"It seems like something closed off just now. You don't have to open back up today — I just want you to know I noticed, and I'm here when you're ready."
Intellectualizing to avoid feeling"You're describing this really clearly — I'm curious what it actually felt like in the moment, underneath the explanation."
"Nothing's going to help anyway""That sounds like real hopelessness talking, not just an opinion about therapy. Can we slow down and look at that feeling directly for a second?" (Treat as clinical content, not a debate to win — assess mood/safety if this is new or intensifying.)
Patient challenges your competence/experience"That's a fair question to ask. Here's what I can tell you about how I'm thinking about this…" (Answer directly and briefly; defensiveness reads as confirmation.)
Patient wants you to just tell them what to do"I have thoughts, and I'll share them — but you know your life better than I do. Let's get there together rather than me handing you an answer that might not fit."

Ending on time — closing summary + eliciting feedback

Closing summary"Let's land it — today we talked about ___, and you're going to try ___ before next time. Does that match what you're taking away?"
Eliciting session feedback"Quick check before you go — did today feel useful, or was there something we should do differently next time?" (Small, cheap, and one of the strongest alliance-building moves available.)
Warm close"I'll see you [date]. If anything comes up before then, you know how to reach us."

What to look for

Progress markers

  • Patient arrives with their own agenda item unprompted — structure has become collaborative, not imposed
  • Sessions land on time without feeling rushed
  • Patient names their own emotional shifts ("I think I just shut down for a second there")
  • Feedback becomes more specific and honest over time — a sign of real trust

Red flags — structural trouble signs

  • Doorknob disclosures every visit → the agenda-setting at minute 0 isn't landing; ask directly earlier
  • You're consistently running over → tighten the bridge-out language, don't just apologize and continue
  • Patient never gives critical feedback → may not feel safe to; ask a more specific question than "how was that"
  • You notice yourself avoiding a topic to protect the schedule → that's a structural failure, not efficiency
