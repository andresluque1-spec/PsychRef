Perinatal Prescribing

Pregnancy and lactation decisions for the agents you actually use. Core principle: untreated maternal illness is itself an exposure — the comparison is never "medication vs. nothing."

Seven principles before any specific drug

  1. Untreated illness has its own fetal exposure: relapsed depression associates with poor prenatal care, substance use, preterm birth, low birth weight, and impaired bonding; relapsed bipolar and psychosis carry higher stakes still. Frame every decision as risk vs. risk.
  2. Relapse math drives the decision: women who discontinue antidepressants in pregnancy relapse at roughly double the rate of those who continue (~68% vs ~26% in the classic Cohen cohort); stopping lithium in bipolar disorder carries very high recurrence risk, worst with abrupt discontinuation.
  3. Never stop abruptly at the positive test. The most common perinatal error happens in a pharmacy parking lot, not a clinic. Organogenesis exposure has already occurred by the time most pregnancies are recognized; abrupt discontinuation adds relapse and withdrawal on top.
  4. Monotherapy at an effective dose beats polypharmacy at timid doses. A subtherapeutic dose exposes the fetus to drug AND illness.
  5. Doses often need to go UP, not down, in late pregnancy — plasma volume, renal clearance, and CYP induction all rise (lamotrigine and lithium most dramatically).
  6. Plan preconception whenever possible. Every woman of childbearing potential on a psychotropic deserves the "what if you became pregnant" conversation before it happens — document it annually.
  7. Use the real references, not package inserts: LactMed (free, NIH), MotherToBaby (866-626-6847), MGH Center for Women's Mental Health (womensmentalhealth.org), Reprotox. Package inserts overstate risk defensively.

Class-wide counseling (know these cold)

  • Neonatal adaptation syndrome: up to ~25–30% of SSRI-exposed newborns — jitteriness, feeding difficulty, mild respiratory distress. Self-limited (days), supportive care only, NOT a reason to taper before delivery (tapering trades a self-limited neonatal syndrome for maternal relapse at the worst possible moment).
  • PPHN: if the risk exists at all, absolute risk remains very small (from ~1–2 to perhaps ~3 per 1000). Present in absolute numbers.
  • Congenital malformations: large datasets show no consistent overall increase for SSRIs as a class; baseline population risk is ~3% and that's the anchor to give.
  • Autism/neurodevelopment: association studies are confounded by indication; sibling-controlled designs largely erase the signal. Say so plainly — patients have read the headlines.

Mood stabilizers — where the real teratogens live

AgentVerdictDetails
ValproateContraindicatedNeural tube defects ~1–2%+, and dose-dependent neurodevelopmental harm (lower IQ, autism risk). Should rarely be prescribed to any woman of childbearing potential without documented counseling + reliable contraception + compelling lack of alternatives. Preconception switch, not first-trimester scramble.
CarbamazepineAvoidNeural tube defects ~1%, plus enzyme induction chaos. High-dose folate if exposure occurred (evidence for prevention is weak but standard).
LithiumUsable with planningEbstein's anomaly risk is real but the classic numbers were inflated: absolute risk roughly 1 in 1,000–2,000 vs ~1 in 20,000 baseline. For many bipolar I patients, continuing lithium is the right call. Fetal echo at 16–20 weeks; levels monthly, then weekly from ~36 weeks (clearance rises, then falls abruptly at delivery); many protocols reduce or hold dose at labor onset and resume postpartum at pre-pregnancy dose with early levels. Postpartum prophylaxis with lithium is among the best-evidenced interventions in psychiatry for preventing postpartum psychosis in bipolar disorder.
LamotriginePreferredReassuring malformation data at usual doses. Critical kinetics: estrogen induces glucuronidation — levels can fall 50%+ across pregnancy, so check a baseline level, monitor, and expect dose increases; then REDUCE stepwise over the first 1–2 postpartum weeks or she'll become toxic (dizziness, diplopia, ataxia) as estrogen falls.

Antipsychotics

  • General rule: for schizophrenia and bipolar maintenance, continue what works. Relapsed psychosis in pregnancy is an emergency; the class data are broadly reassuring for malformations.
  • Quetiapine, olanzapine, risperidone, aripiprazole: most-used, no consistent malformation signal. Olanzapine/quetiapine add gestational-diabetes and weight vigilance — coordinate glucose screening with OB.
  • Third trimester (class-wide): transient neonatal EPS/withdrawal symptoms possible — the FDA class warning. Counsel; don't taper a stable patient for it.
  • Long-acting injectables: if an LAI is what keeps her well, continuing is defensible; the worst outcome is decompensation from a theory-driven switch.

Benzodiazepines & hypnotics

  • Benzodiazepines: the old oral-cleft association looks weak-to-absent in modern data; the real issues are third-trimester dependence — neonatal sedation, withdrawal, rare floppy-infant syndrome — and maternal dependence itself. Lowest effective dose, shortest duration, prefer as-needed over standing; taper toward delivery when feasible.
  • Z-drugs: limited data; behavioral insomnia treatment first — this is exactly where your CBT-I module earns its keep in pregnancy.
  • Hydroxyzine: commonly reached for; avoid in first trimester per conservative guidance and near term (neonatal effects); data thin overall.
  • Buspirone / propranolol: limited but unalarming data; propranolol used widely in obstetrics for other indications.

Postpartum depression & psychosis

  • Screen: EPDS at postpartum contacts (add to your scales tab if not there) — PHQ-9 misses anxiety-flavored presentations.
  • Standard care still works: sertraline remains the workhorse; therapy (your BA module maps perfectly onto the postpartum activity collapse); sleep protection is a treatment, not a luxury — script the partner's night shift like a prescription.
  • Zuranolone (Zurzuvae): oral neuroactive steroid, 14-day course, FDA-approved for postpartum depression — rapid onset (days). Sedation warning (no driving ≥12h post-dose), take with fat-containing food, use contraception during treatment. Access/cost via specialty pharmacy — start the PA early.
  • Postpartum psychosis = emergency. Onset usually days-to-2-weeks postpartum, waxing-waning confusion, mood lability, delusions often infant-centered. This is a psychiatric emergency with real infanticide/suicide risk: same-day hospitalization — your Baker Act page applies if she won't go voluntarily. Strongest single risk factor: bipolar disorder (hence lithium prophylaxis planning in every bipolar pregnancy).

Scripts & documentation

"I found out I'm pregnant — should I stop my medication?" → "First: don't stop anything today, and I'm glad you called before changing anything. Here's the honest picture — being pregnant with untreated depression carries risks too, for you and the baby, so this is never medication-versus-nothing. Your medication is actually one of the best-studied in pregnancy. Let's go through the real numbers together and make this decision once, calmly, instead of in a panic."
