Starting lithium

  • Baseline labs: BMP (Cr/eGFR, electrolytes), TSH, calcium, weight/BMI, pregnancy test when applicable, CBC optional; ECG for age >40 or cardiac history.
  • Dosing: start 300 mg qhs–BID (immediate-release) or 450 mg ER qhs; typical maintenance 900–1200 mg/day. Elderly: start 150–300 mg and expect therapeutic levels at half the adult dose.
  • Once-daily night dosing is preferred when tolerated — at least equivalent efficacy, better adherence, possibly kinder to kidneys (lower average trough exposure), and it makes the morning blood draw a true trough.
  • Formulations: ER smooths GI upset and tremor peaks; IR is cheaper and fine for many. Lithium citrate liquid exists for the pill-averse.
  • Who especially benefits: classic euphoric mania, family history of lithium response, bipolar with prominent suicidality (lithium has anti-suicide evidence no alternative matches), and as augmentation in refractory unipolar depression at lower levels (0.4–0.8).

Levels & monitoring schedule

SituationTarget / action
Acute mania0.8–1.2 mEq/L
Maintenance0.6–0.8 mEq/L (some do well 0.4–0.6, esp. elderly; below 0.4 is rarely protective)
Antidepressant augmentation0.4–0.8 mEq/L
Draw timing12 hours post-dose (± 30 min) — a random level is uninterpretable; with qhs dosing, draw next morning before any dose
After start or dose changeLevel at 5–7 days (steady state), repeat until two stable levels
Stable patientLevel q3–6 months; Cr/eGFR & TSH q6 months; calcium yearly; weight each visit
Elderly, CKD, interacting medsTighten everything: levels q2–3 months, lower targets
Every level gets three questions: When was your last dose before the draw? Any missed or double doses that week? Any new medications — including over-the-counter ibuprofen? An "elevated" level after an 8-hour draw is often just arithmetic.

Toxicity — recognize it by phone

Level*PictureAction
1.5–2.0 (mild)Coarse tremor (vs. baseline fine tremor), nausea/vomiting/diarrhea, lethargy, mild ataxiaHold lithium, hydrate, urgent level + BMP, find the cause (dehydration? new NSAID? dose error?)
2.0–2.5 (moderate)Confusion, dysarthria, worsening ataxia, myoclonus, ECG changesED today — not tomorrow's lab slot
>2.5 (severe)Seizures, stupor/coma, cardiovascular instability, renal failure911; hemodialysis is definitive therapy

*Chronic toxicity occurs at LOWER levels than acute ingestion — an elderly patient can be genuinely toxic at 1.4. Treat the patient, not the number.

Telehealth red flags that mean "stop talking, start arranging transport": new confusion, slurred speech, can't walk straight, vomiting + still taking doses. Classic story: summer landscaping work + diarrhea illness + ibuprofen for the back — that triad is a lithium emergency in Florida heat until proven otherwise.

Interactions that actually happen

Raise levels (danger)Lower levels
Thiazides (classic, can raise 25–40%) · ACE inhibitors/ARBs (insidious, weeks later) · NSAIDs — ibuprofen, naproxen, celecoxib (aspirin and sulindac are the safer exceptions) · dehydration, low-sodium diets, hot-weather volume loss · advancing renal impairment Caffeine (modest) · theophylline · osmotic diuretics · pregnancy (rising clearance) · high sodium intake
  • The pharmacy blind spot: OTC ibuprofen never hits your interaction checker. Tell every lithium patient explicitly: "Tylenol yes, Advil/Aleve no — and remind any doctor who prescribes you a blood-pressure pill or water pill that you take lithium."
  • Neurotoxicity without high levels: lithium + antipsychotics (rarely) and lithium + carbamazepine can produce neurotoxic pictures at "therapeutic" levels — symptoms trump numbers.
  • Serotonergic stacking: lithium is itself serotonergic — count it when tallying serotonin syndrome risk (see the SS/NMS page).

The three organs

Kidney

  • Polyuria/polydipsia (nephrogenic DI): common, dose-related. Confirm (urine volume, morning osmolality), consider once-daily dosing, dose reduction, or amiloride — the specific counter-move.
  • Chronic kidney disease: real but slow, over decades, worse with toxicity episodes. A drifting creatinine buys nephrology involvement and a level-target discussion — usually NOT reflexive discontinuation in a patient whose life lithium is holding together; that decision is a risk-benefit conference, ideally with nephrology and the patient at the table.

Thyroid

  • Hypothyroidism in up to ~20% (women > men). The move is levothyroxine, not stopping lithium. Also watch for lithium unmasking hyperparathyroidism…

Calcium/parathyroid

  • Check calcium yearly: lithium-associated hyperparathyroidism is underdiagnosed — an elevated calcium gets a PTH, and "depression that stopped responding" plus hypercalcemia is a findable, fixable problem.

Sick-day rules (give to every patient)

  • Hold lithium and call us if: vomiting or diarrhea more than once, fever with poor intake, or you can't keep fluids down. Missing two days of lithium is safe; taking lithium into dehydration is not.
  • Florida clause: outdoor work, sports, or beach days in summer heat — drink to a schedule, not to thirst, and never "catch up" missed doses.
  • Never double up after a missed dose. If it's been >6 hours (IR) or you're unsure, skip it.
  • Before surgery or new prescriptions from any other clinician: mention lithium by name.

Documentation anchors

Initiation: Initiated lithium [dose/formulation] for [indication] after review of baseline labs (Cr/eGFR [x], TSH [x], Ca [x]; ECG [done/not indicated, age <40 without cardiac history]). Counseled on toxicity warning signs, sick-day rules, NSAID and dehydration interactions, contraception/pregnancy planning as applicable, and the monitoring schedule. 12-hour trough level ordered for [date, 5–7 days post-initiation].
Maintenance visit: Lithium [dose], last 12-hour level [x] on [date], within target [range] for [indication]. Renal function stable (eGFR [x], [date]); TSH [x]; Ca [x, date]. No new interacting medications including OTC NSAIDs (asked directly). Tremor [status]; polyuria screen [status]. Sick-day rules re-verified. Next level due [date].
The lithium patient's chart should answer, at any moment: last level + date, last Cr/TSH/Ca + dates, current target, and who's watching the kidney trend. If your note template forces those four, lithium becomes one of the safest drugs you prescribe.
